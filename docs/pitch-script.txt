Slide 1, Cover (Yousif): Good morning. We're CTRL ALT Elite: Yousif, Dev, Adam and Abdulrahman. This is Fairview. Before we tell you what it does, we want you to meet someone.

Slide 2, Meet Mo (Yousif): Meet Mo. Mo isn't a real person, but his morning is a real scenario for a lot of people. Mo is a sophomore. At 8:52am he stops at the campus store before class for a quick cup of joe. At 8:53, a camera above the door matches his face to a shoplifting suspect. By 8:56, security has stopped him in front of everyone he knows, when all he was looking for was a quick pick up. Mo had never been in that store. And the camera wasn't broken. It did exactly what it was built to do: always name someone, no matter what.

Slide 3, For some people, this is not fiction (Yousif): For some people, this isn't a fictional story. In 2020, Robert Williams was arrested on his front lawn in Detroit, in front of his wife and daughters, because face-recognition software matched him to a blurry photo of someone else. That same year, Detroit's own police chief said that if they relied on the software alone, it would name the wrong person about 96% of the time. That is techno-racism: technology repeating, or amplifying, the racial bias already in society. And it is a plague that affects us all.

Slide 4, Where today's face recognition fails (Dev): Today's systems fail in six ways. They always name someone. They use one cut-off for everyone. They can't say "not sure". They send faces to the cloud. A photo can fool them. And they keep no record of who decided.

Slide 5, Fairview: four outcomes for every face (Dev): This is Fairview: face verification that asks a person when it is unsure. Every face gets one of four outcomes. Match confirmed. Needs review, where a guard decides. No match, which means a visitor, not a suspect. Or possible spoof: a photo or a screen, not a live face.

Slide 6, What Fairview does differently (Dev): Here's what's different. It asks a person instead of guessing, it checks the face is live, it runs on the device and keeps no video, and it logs every decision. You'll see each of those live in a moment. And it evens out errors across groups without ever looking at race. CTRL ALT Elite maintains and strengthens the idea that facial recognition software doesn't need to use race or skin tone when running.

Slide 7, Live demo and website tour (Adam): Don't believe us? Check it out right now. This is the front desk of a Lenovo office. I'm the security guard, and this camera is the front door. Watch what Fairview says about three people. [Dev steps up, then walks off.] Dev works here. Match confirmed, by name. He walks in, and I never had to look up. [Abdulrahman steps up.] Abdulrahman isn't enrolled, so Fairview doesn't guess a name. It gives him a number. Visitor 1. That number is never saved. He's a visitor, not a suspect. [Yousif steps up. Adam is handed the folder and opens the Review queue.] Yousif has been banned from the premises. Fairview raises an alert, but it never acts on its own. It sends the case here, to the review queue. Every face Fairview isn't sure about lands in this list, with the photo, so a person decides. This folder is his record. Same person. I confirm it. [Adam opens the Activity log and clicks Check integrity.] My decision is now in the activity log, next to every decision the system made. The log is chained together, so if anyone edited a line afterwards, this check would fail. [Adam opens Employees.] This is how people get enrolled. A new hire doesn't enroll once and hope. They check in at the camera on five separate days, so Fairview learns their face in different light, with glasses, after a haircut. Each dot is a day. We enrolled today, so we're on day one of five. [Adam goes back to Live verification. Yousif holds up a phone showing Dev's photo.] Worried about actual criminals just using photos or images of your face? We have thought of that too. Possible spoof. A picture of a face doesn't get you in.

Slide 8, From camera to decision (Abdulrahman): Everything you just saw ran on this laptop. Here's the backend. A camera frame comes in and stays in memory. We find the face. We check that it's live and clear enough to judge. Two independent face models, AdaFace and ArcFace, each give an opinion, and we combine them into one score, calibrated so error rates even out across groups. That score leads to one of the four outcomes. The confident ones go straight to the activity log, which is hash-chained so nobody can quietly change it. The unsure ones go to the guard first, and the guard's decision is logged too.

Slide 9, Where we are today (Abdulrahman): In our fairness test, a conventional system falsely matched its worst-served skin-type group 5.9 times as often as its best-served. Fairview: 2.7 times. To be clear, that's simulated on synthetic data, not measured on real people.

Lenovo is the world's largest PC maker, about one in four PCs sold, so it already supplies the companies that would buy this. And Lenovo has made responsible AI a public promise. Fairview is a product that shows it. The first pilot could be an hour from this room, at Lenovo's US headquarters in Morrisville.

Slide 11, Close (Yousif): Mo's camera was built to always name someone. Fairview is built to know when it shouldn't. Easy to understand. Easy to implement: it runs on hardware you already buy, with no cloud contract. Thank you. We'd love your questions.
